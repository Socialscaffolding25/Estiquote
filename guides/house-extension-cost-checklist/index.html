House extension costs: what to put in your budget

The useful starting question is not just “what does an extension cost per square metre?” It is “what must this price include to deliver the space I actually want?”

Define the finished space before comparing prices

A weatherproof shell and a finished kitchen extension are different scopes. Write down whether your project needs a fitted kitchen, heating, lighting, flooring, decorating and work to the existing house. Ask anyone providing an estimate to state where their price stops.

Area matters, but it does not describe the whole job. A smaller extension can still need site setup, drainage alterations and a major opening into the existing house. A single cost-per-square-metre figure cannot describe those differences.

Four questions that can change the scope

  1. How does the new space connect? Record openings and alterations you want. A structural professional should establish the design needed; an app cannot confirm a beam or foundation.
  2. How will materials reach the site? Tell builders about restricted side access, parking and the space available for storage.
  3. How much glass do you want? Describe door and window sizes and intended specification early, rather than leaving a vague “doors included” line.
  4. What happens to the existing rooms? Include the old kitchen removal, changed layouts and making good where relevant.

Keep approvals separate from design assumptions

Planning permission and building regulations approval are different matters. GOV.UK’s building regulations guide explains the distinction and links to the different arrangements in Scotland and Northern Ireland. Confirm the route for your property with the relevant authority or adviser before proceeding.

Build the budget without counting items twice

Start with the agreed construction scope. Add separately priced items that are excluded, professional costs not already included, applicable tax and a contingency for uncertainty. Keep optional upgrades on a separate list.

Do not add a kitchen, design fee or site allowance again if it is already within a total. Mark every budget line as “included”, “separate” or “not confirmed”. Our renovation budget planner gives you a worksheet for doing this.

How to use an extension cost estimator

  1. Choose extension, not a full new build. Select the country and area type for the project. Check the scope label before entering measurements.
  2. Use a consistent area. For a simple rectangular planning example, 5 metres by 6 metres is 30 m². Real layouts may need separate areas measured and added together. Check the measurement basis with the person preparing your drawings; an area is not evidence of buildability.
  3. Record the finish and extras. Keep these choices with the result so you know what changed when comparing another scenario. Confirm what is outside the guide, including tax and the separately held contingency.
  4. Turn uncertainty into questions. Take the checklist to builders and ask them to state the assumptions behind their prices. A modelled budget range is the starting point for that conversation.

Common extension budgeting questions

Can I budget from a cost per square metre alone?

Use it as an early sense-check, not a complete scope. The area does not tell you the foundation design, glazing specification, access arrangements or work to existing rooms. Ask what the rate includes before multiplying it by your floor area.

Does an extension estimate include a fitted kitchen?

Do not assume it does. Ask whether the price includes supply, fitting, appliances, worktops and service connections. If the extension creates a kitchen, use the kitchen renovation budget worksheet alongside the building-work checklist and avoid counting the same item twice.

Is the lowest builder quote the best budget?

Only comparable scopes make meaningful price comparisons. Keep unpriced exclusions visible and ask for clarification before choosing. A quote’s position against the app’s range does not prove value, workmanship or suitability.
